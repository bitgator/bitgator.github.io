ContextPM

AI-powered product management — from raw feedback to development-ready stories.

  • Teams collect feedback from dozens of sources but can't synthesize it
  • AI belongs in the pipeline, not bolted on as a chatbot
  • Every stage transition should be a deliberate PM decision
  • The system should never auto-promote

Most AI-in-product-management tools treat the LLM as a chatbot sitting next to your backlog. ContextPM does something different: it models the full PM workflow as a four-stage pipeline with explicit status cycles, clear ownership, and defined handoffs. Feedback flows in from connectors — Zendesk, Aha!, manual entry. AI clusters and synthesizes it into recommendations. PMs curate those into ideas. Validated ideas become development stories.

The critical design rule: the system never auto-promotes. Every stage transition is a deliberate PM decision. AI handles synthesis and structure; humans handle judgment and prioritization. This isn't a philosophical position — it's a direct response to watching teams lose control when AI tools start making decisions on their behalf.

Stories are opinionated by design. The title is framed as an end state — "Partners can X" not "Create X feature." Each story includes an As a/I want/So that statement, assumptions, open questions, and use cases pulled from the actual feedback that spawned it. Architecture decision

Step Functions over browser execution

The three-pass recommendation pipeline can take minutes across hundreds of feedback items. Browser-based execution dies on navigation, laptop sleep, or network interruption. AWS Step Functions survive all of these, provide native retry with backoff, and produce a durable execution log. Worth the complexity for reliability.

Product decision

Preservation-based reprocessing

When rerunning recommendation generation, starred and promoted recommendations survive — everything else is deleted and regenerated. PMs invest real effort curating recommendations. A full rerun shouldn't destroy that work, but non-curated output should be regenerable as new feedback arrives.

Constraint as feature

Mantine with zero custom CSS

All styling uses Mantine component props and theme tokens. No CSS modules, no styled-components, no inline pixel values. This eliminates an entire category of tech debt — every spacing value, color, and typography choice flows through the theme, making visual consistency automatic and redesigns mechanical.

Stack

Solo builder, full stack

React 19, TypeScript, Mantine v8, AWS Amplify Gen 2, DynamoDB, AppSync GraphQL, Step Functions, Lambda. Built end-to-end by one person to prove the concept and discover the product before investing in a team. Currently dogfooding it on its own roadmap.

CloudRadial

Mac App & System Daemon

A product director who prototypes at the native platform level — not just web — because the product problem demanded it.

  • Product leaders who prototype make better decisions
  • Platform-native UX unlocks what web can't touch
  • System daemons require understanding OS constraints
  • API design drives partner ecosystem value

CloudRadial's ChatAI product needed a macOS presence that went beyond a browser tab. I built a native menubar application in Swift that integrates with the ChatAI API, giving managed service providers quick access to AI-assisted support without context-switching away from their workflow.

Alongside the menubar app, I built a system-level daemon for endpoint reporting — the kind of always-on background process that web technologies fundamentally can't deliver. This wasn't an engineering exercise for its own sake. It was a product decision: understanding what the platform can do shapes what the product should offer.

A product leader who can prototype at this level doesn't need to wait for an engineering spike to validate feasibility. They walk into the planning meeting knowing what's possible, what's hard, and what's not worth the effort — because they've already touched it.

Strategic value

Feasibility through craft

Building a working prototype answered questions an engineering spike would have taken weeks to surface. The product roadmap was better for it — decisions were grounded in what the platform actually supports.

Product judgment

Knowing what not to ship

The prototype revealed which features belonged in a native app and which belonged in the web product. Not everything benefits from native — the exercise clarified the boundary.

CloudRadial

ChatAI Dialog Flow & AI Integration

Designing where AI belongs in a conversation — and where it doesn't.

  • Not every turn needs AI
  • Scripted flows for known paths; AI for ambiguity
  • Connectors extend AI per partner context
  • The seams should be invisible to users

The hardest problem in conversational AI isn't making the AI smarter — it's deciding when to use it at all. ChatAI's dialog system intertwines scripted conversation flows with specific agentic AI components, and the product challenge was designing the seams between them.

Scripted flows handle predictable, high-stakes paths where consistency matters: ticket creation, escalation routing, data collection. AI handles the ambiguous middle — interpreting intent, suggesting next steps, summarizing context. The architecture ensures the user experience feels unified even though the underlying system shifts between deterministic and probabilistic modes.

The Connector model was designed to extend AI capabilities on a per-partner basis. Each managed service provider operates a different stack, different processes, different terminology. Rather than building one monolithic AI, the Connector architecture lets partners plug in their own context — their documentation, their workflows, their escalation rules — so the AI operates with domain fluency, not just generic competence.

The product skill isn't making AI do more — it's designing the system so AI does less, in exactly the right places.

Systems design

Scripted + agentic hybrid

Every dialog flow maps decision points where the system chooses between a scripted path and an AI-driven response. The architecture is explicit about which turns are deterministic and which are probabilistic.

Platform thinking

The Connector model

AI capabilities extend per partner through a pluggable Connector architecture. Each partner's domain knowledge, tooling, and workflows become inputs to the AI — turning generic intelligence into contextual expertise.

On Scaling AI-Enabled Development

Vibe-coding works beautifully for solo builders. It breaks down the moment you add a second person to the room.

When I'm building alone — ContextPM, for example — AI-assisted development is transformative. I can move from idea to working code in hours. The feedback loop is tight: I hold the full context, I make every tradeoff, and the AI accelerates execution without introducing coordination overhead. This is what people mean by "vibe-coding," and for solo builders, it's genuinely powerful.

But scale that to a cross-functional team and the model fractures. The reason isn't technical — it's that everyone on the team knows different things. A product manager, a designer, a QA engineer, and a backend developer each hold different domain expertise. They see different risks, apply different quality bars, and optimize for different outcomes. When a solo builder uses AI, all of that expertise is collapsed into one person's judgment. On a team, it's distributed — and AI doesn't know how to navigate the distribution.

The judgment distribution problem

An AI coding assistant that writes a feature for a solo founder is operating in a context where one person defines "done." On a team, "done" is a negotiation between the PM who scoped it, the designer who shaped the interaction, the engineer who'll maintain it, and the QA lead who'll break it. AI-generated code that passes one person's bar may violate three others' constraints — constraints the AI was never given.

I've spent four months unwinding poor decisions made with AI assistance on a team. Not because the AI was wrong, but because it was right within one person's context and wrong within the team's. The code compiled. The tests passed. The architecture was a dead end that only became visible when someone from a different discipline looked at it.

What actually works

The answer isn't restricting AI — it's encoding the team's domain expertise into the workflow before AI gets involved. This means explicit specification layers that capture not just requirements but constraints from each discipline. It means treating AI-generated output as a draft that requires cross-functional review, not a finished artifact. And it means building systems — like ContextPM's two-layer model — where the human decision layer and the AI execution layer have separate contracts with separate audiences.

The product leaders who will thrive in AI-enabled organizations aren't the ones who code the fastest with Copilot. They're the ones who design the systems that bring every discipline's judgment into the AI-assisted workflow — without losing speed, without losing quality, and without losing the human expertise that makes the product worth building.
